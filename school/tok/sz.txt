How reliably can an isolated knower construct and justify knowledge using only the internal logic of a formal system?

Stefan Zweig's Schachnovelle provides an in depth exploration of the protagonist operating within a closed axiomatic framework. The protagonist, Dr. B, is subjected to total sensory deprivation by the Gestapo. In order not to lose his sanity, he memorizes a book of master chess games. This transforms his mind into a closed formal system. Chess is governed by finite and absolute rules which is why this source is amazing for evaluating the nature of knowledge.

The core problem of knowledge here is the crisis of justification in perfect isolation. Dr. B demonstrates that an isolated knower can reliably construct vast amounts of deductive knowledge. He generates thousands of new chess matches purely in his imagination. He functions like a recursive algorithm exploring a complex chess positions. A critical problem arises when he attempts to justify this knowledge. In mathematics and formal logic, knowledge relies heavily on external validation, such as peer reviews by a community of knowers, to ensure objective truth.

Without this external reality or physical opponent, he is forced to split his consciousness, acting as both the creator of the move (the player controlling the white pieces) and the validator (the player controlling the black pieces). Lacking the objective feedback provided by a community of knowers, Dr. B's internal logic loses its base case, collapsing into an infinite and destructive feedback loop, which the author labels as "chess poisoning."

This demonstrates that while isolated axiomatic systems can effectively generate internally valid knowledge, they rely on external peer review to objectively justify the knowledge.
